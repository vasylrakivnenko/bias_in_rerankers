\begin{abstract}
\noindent

Automated hiring pipelines can retrieve candidate documents with an embedding model and then reorder the leading candidates with a neural reranker. Prior audits have studied gender-associated differences in retrieval, while evidence about the reranking stage remains limited. We audit 14 open and proprietary rerankers with 8,320 counterfactual document pairs per model. Within each pair, qualifications are unchanged and only the selected name and pronouns differ. All 14 models favor the male-associated document more often for male-typed occupations than for female-typed occupations. In synthetic pools of 20 equally qualified candidates, one retrieval-and-reranking pipeline assigns 79\% of top-3 positions to male-associated documents; behind the same retriever, the audited rerankers range from 44\% to 85\%, against 60\% for retrieval alone. Results vary materially across models, occupations, names, templates, and query wordings. We then test a simple input transform that replaces names with one placeholder and gendered pronouns with one uniform token before ranking. The transform makes each counterfactual pair textually identical and therefore removes score differences attributable to those markers by construction. On a near-ceiling synthetic occupation-matching check, top-rank accuracy is 827 of 832 before and 830 of 832 after transformation, a difference not distinguishable from zero. These findings establish sensitivity to the tested names and pronouns, rather than discrimination in deployed hiring or a complete solution to demographic bias.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Modern search systems commonly retrieve a candidate set with a fast first-stage method, such as lexical matching or a dense embedding model \cite{karpukhin2020}, and then use a more expensive model to rerank that set \cite{nogueira2019}. The same architecture can be used for job and resume matching. A candidate must therefore pass two learned ordering stages before appearing near the top of a recruiter's list.

Evidence of social bias in word representations and retrieval is well established \cite{caliskan2017,rakivnenko2024,wilson2024}. Neural rankers can also amplify gendered patterns in retrieved content \cite{rekabsaz2020,rekabsaz2021}. Resume-screening studies report effects in different directions across tasks and models \cite{an2025,wilson2024}. Yet a broad counterfactual audit of current rerankers is missing, even though a reranker can reverse the order produced by retrieval.

We ask three narrow empirical questions. First, do current rerankers score otherwise identical professional documents differently when selected names and pronouns change? Second, how do such differences affect a two-stage shortlist? Third, which simple de-identification rules remove the measured difference without reducing performance on a basic relevance check?

Our contribution is a dated, reproducible audit rather than a claim about any employer's deployed system. We compare 14 rerankers on a controlled synthetic corpus, propagate the scores through a retrieval-and-reranking simulation, and test de-identification on four locally runnable rerankers. The design isolates sensitivity to the changed text. It does not isolate gender from every other association carried by a name, and it does not measure actual hiring decisions.

\section{Experimental design}

\subsection{Counterfactual documents}

Each observation is a pair of short professional documents. The two versions describe the same occupation, experience, skills, and accomplishments. One version uses a male-associated given name and masculine pronouns; the other uses a female-associated given name and feminine pronouns. For a reranker that scores each document independently, any within-pair score difference is therefore caused by the changed name and pronoun tokens. The API rerankers received both versions of a pair in the same request, always with the male-associated version first; vendors do not guarantee that a document's score is independent of its position in a request. A constant position effect would shift male-typed and female-typed occupations alike, so it cannot produce the contrast between them, but it could move a model's overall level. Because names can also signal age, ethnicity, nationality, and individual identity, the design cannot attribute the whole difference to gender alone.

We combine 4 document templates, 4 query phrasings, and 10 pairs of common American given names. Names come from national birth-name records \cite{ssa2026}. The complete templates, queries, and names appear in Appendix A.

We initially score 82 occupations. We label occupations with the share of women employed in the corresponding federal labor-force category \cite{bls2025}. We retain 52 occupations with a clear label: 18 male-typed, 21 female-typed, and 13 close to evenly split. The remaining 30 lack a usable match, have a suppressed estimate, or fall in an intentionally ambiguous range. The released repository records every inclusion and exclusion, its federal category, and the exclusion reason.

\subsection{Rerankers and analysis}

We evaluate 14 rerankers from commercial APIs and open-weight releases. The scores were collected on 2026-02-24. A further endpoint, semantic-ranker-default-003, returned scores identical to semantic-ranker-default-004 on every row; we treat the two as one model and exclude the duplicate from every reported figure. Model identifiers and results appear in Appendix B. The released provenance table records each access route, score range, overall tie rate, and collection date.

For each model and occupation group, we report whether the male-associated version scores higher, the versions tie, or the female-associated version scores higher. When a single directional share is needed, a tie contributes one half to each side. Ties largely reflect score resolution rather than indifference: several models return coarsely rounded scores, and one open-weight reranker, run in bfloat16, emits only 254 distinct values across 16,640 scored documents. Confidence intervals use a seeded cluster bootstrap that resamples occupations, because comparisons within an occupation are not independent. Scores from different models have different scales, so we compare directions and within-model gaps rather than raw magnitudes across models.

\subsection{Two-stage shortlist simulation}

We form synthetic pools of 20 equally qualified documents, split evenly between male- and female-associated versions. The retriever BAAI/bge-large-en-v1.5 selects the top 10 candidates. The reranker BAAI/bge-reranker-v2-m3 then produces a top-3 shortlist. We also evaluate the retriever and reranker separately and repeat the pipeline with every audited reranker behind the same retriever. The simulation reuses the reranker scores from the single-stage audit rather than rescoring each pool. This is exact for a reranker that scores documents independently; for the API rerankers, which scored each document inside a larger per-query request, it is an assumption. Because candidate qualifications are identical by construction, departures from an even shortlist reflect ordering effects from the changed markers and tie handling.

\subsection{De-identification}

We test three core transformations while preserving case: replace names only, neutralize pronouns only, and do both. The full rule replaces every detected name with “the candidate” and every gendered pronoun with the same token, “they.” Appendix E adds four comparison conditions: lowercasing alone, a control that removes no marker; two naive grammatical rewrites that also replace names and map “she/he” to “they,” “him” to “them,” “his” to “their,” and “her” to either “their” or “them”; and a following-word rule, otherwise identical, that maps “her” to “their” or “them” depending on the next word. This deliberately simple normalization is applied before scoring. The two counterfactual versions then become the same string, so any deterministic text-only embedding model or reranker must assign them the same representation or score. This is a symmetry guarantee, not an empirical discovery. Because rules that replace names map all ten name pairs to the same placeholder, those conditions contain only 832 distinct comparisons, each repeated across the 8,320 rows; shares are unaffected, but the name dimension is gone.

We run the transformations on four open-weight rerankers. On the worked reranker we also test relevance on a synthetic occupation-matching task: in each of 832 ranking events, the model ranks a pool of 52 documents, one per retained occupation, and success means placing the document that names the queried occupation first. Every document states its occupation, and the transforms never touch that term, so the task is near ceiling by design. It checks that the transform does not destroy the occupation signal; it cannot measure retained ranking quality, and it does not test whether a system selects the best-qualified person within an occupation.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Reranker scores track occupational gender composition}

All 14 audited models favor the male-associated document more often for male-typed occupations than for female-typed occupations. Across models, the median tie-aware male-favored share is 79\% on male-typed occupations and 37\% on female-typed occupations; the two medians are taken separately and need not come from the same model. Within a single model, the median gap between the two categories is 32 percentage points (range 14 to 58). The size varies across models, but the direction of this difference is consistent.

Evenly split occupations reveal substantial model variation. The median male-favored share is 63\%, but 10 models lie above an even split and 4 lie below it. For 3 models, the occupation-clustered confidence interval includes an even split. A deployer therefore cannot infer either the size or direction of a model's behavior without testing that model.

The result is not invariant to design choices. Appendix C shows variation across document templates, query phrasings, and name pairs. Variation across names is especially large. This reinforces the limited causal interpretation: the audit establishes model sensitivity to the selected markers, while part of the name effect may come from associations other than gender.

\subsection{The reranker can dominate the pipeline}

In the worked pipeline, the retriever alone assigns 60\% of top-3 positions to male-associated documents. The reranker alone assigns 79\%, and the combined pipeline assigns 79\%. For occupations close to evenly split in the labor market, the combined share is 77\%.

The interaction depends on occupation type. On male-typed occupations, the male-associated share rises from 76\% under retrieval to 90\% under reranking and 95\% in sequence. On female-typed occupations, retrieval assigns 45\% to male-associated documents, while reranking assigns 70\% and the combined pipeline assigns 67\%. The two stages can therefore compound or counteract one another.

Appendix D repeats the simulation with every audited reranker behind the same retriever. The resulting shortlist share ranges from 44\% to 85\%, and 6 rerankers produce a shortlist with a lower male-associated share than retrieval alone (60\%). The worked example sits near the top of that range rather than being typical. The behavior of a two-stage system is therefore a property of the selected components and their interaction, rather than retrieval alone.

\subsection{Full marker removal eliminates the measured pathway}

Removing only names does not remove the difference. On the worked reranker, the male-associated version wins 98.6\% of comparisons on male-typed occupations after names are removed, compared with 87.0\% before. The mean absolute gap is essentially unchanged (0.036 to 0.038; paired occupation-clustered 95\% interval on the difference [-0.0005, 0.0047]). Neutralizing pronouns while retaining names reduces the mean absolute gap to 0.024 (interval on the difference [-0.0147, -0.0101]). Partial removal is not consistent across the four rerankers in Appendix E: removing names alone leaves the mean absolute gap unchanged or higher on two models and reduces it on the other two, and on one model it lowers the male-favored share where the worked reranker's rises. Partial de-identification therefore has unpredictable effects.

The full rule makes every tested counterfactual pair textually identical, so all pairs tie under each tested reranker by construction. Identical strings are scored once and the score is reused, so this tie rate is not an observation; it holds for any reranker whose scores are deterministic. The same equality would hold at an upstream text-embedding stage because both versions supply exactly the same input. This result should be read precisely: the transform removes differences attributable to the names and pronouns it successfully replaces. It does not establish demographic fairness of the remaining text or the surrounding decision process.

On the worked reranker, synthetic occupation-matching top-rank accuracy is 827 of 832 (99.4\%) before the full transform and 830 of 832 (99.8\%) after it, a difference that is not distinguishable from zero (exact McNemar p = 0.25); top-three accuracy is 100\% in both conditions. Because the task is near ceiling, this shows only that the transform leaves the occupation signal intact. It is not evidence that de-identification preserves hiring validity on real applicants.

\section{Implications and future work}

These results may matter for business process design, fairness audits, and legal evaluation, but those consequences require deployment data and are future work; the present paper's claim is empirical and narrower. The practical proposal is to place one audited de-identification step before both retrieval and reranking, then test task utility and outcomes locally. Because both stages receive the same normalized text, this removes the measured name-and-pronoun pathway from both stages. It may also reduce one source of repeated exclusion when many employers reuse the same ranking components, connecting our study to the “algorithmic monoculture” described by Bommasani and colleagues \cite{bommasani2026}. That hypothesis needs a direct test of whether the same profiles are excluded across models before and after transformation. Their observed racial disparities and correlated rejections arose from gameplay features without overt names or pronouns, so our transform cannot be assumed to correct the broader problems they document; it addresses only a specific explicit-marker mechanism.

\section{Limitations}

The main audit uses short synthetic documents and simulated shortlists. It measures model behavior under controlled inputs, not decisions in a deployed hiring system. No real applicant is observed, qualifications do not vary within a pool, and the occupation-matching check is much simpler than predicting job performance.

Names carry several social signals at once. Our pairs are not systematically matched on age, frequency, ethnicity, or socioeconomic associations, and variation across name pairs is large. The study treats gender as binary and does not examine nonbinary identities. It also does not audit race or intersectional effects.

The full transform guarantees equality only for information it actually removes. Real resumes may signal gender through schools, affiliations, activities, job histories, and writing patterns \cite{parasurama2022full}. Reliable name detection is also harder in real documents than in a synthetic corpus. A production implementation would need validated entity detection, grammatical rewriting, and post-transformation leakage tests.

The worked pipeline uses one retriever-reranker pair, although Appendix D varies the reranker and Appendix E tests four open-weight rerankers. Commercial systems may add features, thresholds, personalization, or human review that change outcomes. The results are a dated snapshot of model versions queried on 2026-02-24.

A companion experiment sampled 1,952 distinct documents from a public corpus of 2,483 published résumé examples scraped from a career website (released under CC0). These are not real applicants' documents, and the publisher had already removed most names, so 2,182 of the counterfactual pairs were built by inserting a name rather than swapping an existing marker. Its model directions and magnitudes differ from the synthetic results. Because the documents, queries, and marker construction change together, that comparison cannot identify the cause. We retain it as a warning against generalizing the synthetic effect size to applicant résumés in deployment. Its results predate the released version of its script, and its corpus must be rebuilt from the repository's provenance notes to reproduce it.

\section{Conclusion}

Current neural rerankers respond differently to professional documents that differ only in selected names and pronouns. Across 14 models, the direction of that response consistently changes with occupational gender composition, while the baseline direction varies by product. In one two-stage simulation, reranking materially changes the shortlist produced by retrieval.

A simple normalization of names and gendered pronouns eliminates this particular pairwise difference because it makes the compared inputs identical. A near-ceiling occupation-matching check is unaffected, but real hiring utility and residual demographic leakage remain untested. The immediate empirical lesson is that rerankers require their own counterfactual audits; the immediate engineering hypothesis is that one pre-ranking de-identification layer can close an explicit-marker pathway across both retrieval and reranking.
